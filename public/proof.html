Does it make money? A simulation

The demo week loses money: $285.00 paid revenue against $326.50 ad spend. What if the shop kept the same weekly budget for 8 weeks and moved it each week? The evidence is thin (6 paid orders across 4 ads), so each of 2,000 runs draws every ad's true paid rate from what the export and PayPal show, and the policies never see that draw; they learn from noisy weeks as a shop would. Three policies: keep the split; follow leads per dollar, which is what ad platforms optimise for; follow PayPal-confirmed paid orders per dollar with the dashboard's own rule (an ad moves only after 3 paid orders, every ad keeps 10%). Figures are paid revenue minus ad spend, before product cost, so not profit. A simulation, not a measurement; it reruns on a real shop's numbers with node scripts/eval-budget-sim.mjs shop.csv.

Budget decisions, on weeks with known answers

The demo shop has too few paid orders for any budget move, so five synthetic weeks (labelled SYNTHETIC wherever they appear; none of their payments is a PayPal capture) test the decision itself: enough orders on two ads, refunds hiding behind captures, a double payment, the ad with the most leads and the least money, and too few orders everywhere. Too few orders to move money: what it says instead

Most small shops live here. The demo shop has 6 paid orders across 4 ads, so the rule above keeps every budget. Keeping is not the whole answer: for each ad the ledger works out, in code (paybackOdds in src/ledger.js), the chance its paid revenue covers its ad spend, a 90% range for its cost per paid order, and, while that is still unclear, how much more spend with no paid order would make "below its ad spend" 90% likely. The model is Gamma-Poisson: paid orders per dollar, a prior worth one order at the shop's own cost per paid order, and the shop's average paid order as the break-even. The budget agent quotes these numbers through the same evidence gate; it cannot make one up. The dashboard shows them in the "Covers its spend?" column.

Why this exists: the same gap in the author's own ads

The demo shop is fictional, so here is a real case: the author's own Meta ads sending traffic to Shopee affiliate links in Vietnam, 2 to 8 October 2026, the days the merchant platform's export covers, 33 ads. Clicks and spend come from Meta's API per ad; orders and commission from Shopee's official export, matched to each ad one-to-one by its link's Sub_id. Not PayPal: it is why the author built it, shown where the author had real numbers, and below it is run through Paid Signal's own budget rule. Motivation, not a result.

A small test (about $43), so it shows a pattern, not a result: budget that follows the ad platform's number can go to the wrong ad. Raw numbers: real-ads-case.json.

Through Paid Signal's budget rule (node scripts/eval-real-case.mjs, completed orders standing in for PayPal-confirmed payments, matched to ads by Sub_id as a capture is by its custom_id): all 5 completed orders came from the three ads ranked 7th, 9th and 10th of 10 by clicks per unit of spend (the 24 ads with no order counted as one row). A rule that follows clicks would raise the Bag ad, which completed no order, and cut Fashion item E, which completed one. Paid Signal's rule moves nothing: no ad has 3 completed orders yet, so it keeps the split. Its small-sample read is blunter: every row is very likely below its ad spend (the highest chance of covering it is about 2 in a billion), so the honest answer was to stop the test.
